Most marketing starts in the wrong place. Someone asks, "Should we be on TikTok?" or "Do we need a new logo?" Those are tactics. They answer a question nobody has asked yet.

The question is this: what does winning look like for you this year?

We ask it at the start of every engagement. Here's how to answer it yourself, in four steps. We'll use two made-up examples along the way: a construction firm and a nonprofit.

## Step 1: Write one goal for the year

Pick one outcome that would make this year a win. Not five. One.

A good marketing goal has three parts:

- **A result you can count.** Calls, bid requests, signed contracts, donors, event signups.
- **A number.** Pick one you'd be proud of and could defend.
- **A deadline.** Usually the end of the year.

"Grow our brand" isn't a goal. You can't tell whether you hit it.

Say you run a construction firm that mostly does residential remodels. You want more commercial work. Your goal might be: sign six commercial contracts by December. Everything else in the plan exists to serve that number.

Now picture a nonprofit that runs an after-school program and depends on individual giving. Its goal might be: grow monthly donors from 80 to 150 by year end.

If you can't choose, ask which goal would make the others easier. That's usually the one.

## Step 2: Pick the audience that decides

Every goal has a person on the other side of it. Name them as specifically as you can.

For the construction firm, the audience isn't "businesses." It's owners and facility managers of small commercial properties within an hour of the office. It might also be the architects who recommend contractors.

For the nonprofit, it isn't "people who care about kids." It's local parents, churches and small business owners. They already believe in the mission. Nobody has asked them to give monthly.

Write down three things about your audience:

1. What problem are they trying to solve?
2. Where do they already spend their attention?
3. What would make them trust you enough to act?

If you can't answer those yet, start with conversations. Call five past clients or donors. Ask how they found you and why they said yes.

## Step 3: Choose two or three channels

This is where most plans fall apart. There are dozens of places to market, and it's tempting to be everywhere. A small team spread across eight channels can't do any of them well.

Pick two or three that match your audience and your goal.

The construction firm might choose:

- **Its website**, with a clear page for commercial work and a simple way to request a bid.
- **Search ads** aimed at people looking for commercial contractors nearby.
- **Direct outreach** to property managers, backed by a sharp sales sheet.

The nonprofit might choose:

- **Email**, because monthly giving is built on relationships.
- **Social media**, with short videos of the program in action.
- **One annual event** with a clear ask to join as a monthly donor.

Notice what's missing. The construction firm doesn't need to post every day. The nonprofit doesn't need search ads to start. Saying no to a channel is a decision, not a failure.

## Step 4: Decide how you'll measure

A goal without a scoreboard turns into a feeling. Decide now what you'll track and how often you'll look.

Keep it simple:

- **The goal number.** Contracts signed. Monthly donors added.
- **One or two early signals.** Bid requests from the website. Email signups after the event.
- **Where each lead came from.** Ask on every form and every first call. A CRM makes this easier, but a spreadsheet works to start.

Review it monthly. If a channel isn't producing after a fair test, change it. If it is, give it more attention. The plan should change as you learn. The goal shouldn't.

## Put it on one page

When you're done, you should have a single page:

- The goal for the year, with a number and a deadline.
- The audience, described in a few sentences.
- The two or three channels, and what you'll do in each.
- The numbers you'll track, and when you'll review them.

That page is your marketing plan. Every design request, video idea or new platform gets measured against it. Does this help us hit the goal? If not, it waits.
